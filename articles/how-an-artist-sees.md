# How an artist sees

### Breaking an image down: subject, structure, value, colour, light, mark

**Kellie Patry · KPHD Studio, Los Angeles · 8 November 2026 · working draft v0.1**

> **Where this sits.** The studio has been mapping three ways of reading the same image. The language model's map is the read map: a path of read first, read next, stop when, write back. The architect's map is 5D and fascia: the model whose components carry their own information, and the typed links that carry change between records. This paper is the third map, the artist's. It is a method you can run on any image, a worksheet to run it with, and a translation of each reading into the room. The last section sets the three maps side by side.

---

## 1. The one idea under all of it

An artist does not see things. An artist sees relations.

A colour is not a colour. It is warmer or cooler than the colour beside it, lighter or darker, duller or cleaner. A shape is a shape only against the shape it sits on. A line is fast or slow only compared with the lines around it. Josef Albers built a whole course on this: the same grey reads as two different greys on two different grounds, and the eye cannot be argued out of it. [1] John Berger put the same point another way. We never look at one thing. We look at the relation between things and ourselves. [2]

So the artist's method is a way of making relations visible. It goes from the whole to the parts and back. First the whole image at a distance, as a few big shapes. Then the parts, one dimension at a time. Then back to the whole, to ask whether the parts explain the first impression.

Two habits make the method work.

**Squint.** Half close your eyes until detail disappears. What is left is the value structure: three to five masses of light and dark. If the image still reads when squinted, it has a structure. If it turns to mud, no amount of colour or detail will save it.

**Name before you judge.** Art teachers borrow a four-step order from Edmund Feldman: describe, analyse, interpret, judge. [3] Describe what is literally there. Analyse how it is arranged. Interpret what it means. Only then say whether it works. Most people start at step four. The artist earns step four by doing the first three.

## 2. The order of looking

Below are nine dimensions in the order an artist tends to reach for them. They do not happen in sequence in a trained eye. They happen at once. The sequence is for learning to hold them all.

### 2.1 Subject: what it is of, and what it is about

Three questions, in order, following Erwin Panofsky's three levels of reading a picture. [4]

- **What is literally there.** A woman, a table, a window, a bowl of lemons. No interpretation yet. This is the pre-iconographic read.
- **What those things are.** The window is a studio window. The lemons sit on a Delft plate. The woman is reading a letter. Now the objects have names and histories. This is the iconographic read.
- **What the picture is about.** Not lemons. Waiting, perhaps. Or interior light as a stand-in for an interior life. This is the iconological read, and it is where the artist's intent lives.

The gap between the first and third answers is the whole work. A still life of lemons is never about lemons.

Also ask: **where is the viewer standing?** Eye level, above, below, inside the scene or outside it. The viewpoint is a decision about who you are in the picture.

### 2.2 Composition: the armature

The composition is the skeleton the image hangs on. Look for:

- **Format.** Portrait, landscape, square, panoramic. The format is the first constraint and the first decision.
- **Division.** Where the big lines fall. Thirds, halves, the golden section, a diagonal. Arthur Wesley Dow taught composition as the division of a rectangle into pleasing proportions before any subject enters. [5]
- **Focal point.** Where the eye lands first. Usually the place of highest contrast, sharpest edge, cleanest colour, or where lines converge.
- **Eye path.** Where the eye goes next, and next, and whether it is led back in or allowed to leave. A good image is a route, not a display.
- **Figure and ground.** What reads as object and what reads as space. Then look at the ground as shapes in its own right. The negative shapes are designed too, or they are not.
- **Edges.** Hard, soft, or lost. A hard edge stops the eye. A soft edge lets it slide. A lost edge, where two values merge, joins two forms into one. Edges are how an artist controls attention without adding anything.
- **Scale and cropping.** How much of the subject is shown, how close, what has been cut off at the frame. Cropping is the most underrated decision in any image.
- **Balance.** Symmetrical, asymmetrical, radial. Whether the visual weight sits where the format can carry it.

### 2.3 Value: light and dark

Value is the most important dimension and the one most people skip, because colour is louder.

- **Value structure.** Squint. Count the masses. Two, three, four, five? Map them. The Japanese term *notan*, brought into Western teaching by Dow, means exactly this: the arrangement of light and dark as a pattern, before colour. [5]
- **Key.** High key, mostly light values. Low key, mostly dark. Full range, both ends. The key is the first thing that sets a mood.
- **Range and contrast.** How far apart the lightest light and darkest dark are, and where they touch. The point where they touch is almost always the focal point.
- **Proportion.** How much of the picture is light, mid, dark. A picture that is one third each is rarely interesting. Dominance makes a picture.

### 2.4 Colour: hue, value, chroma, and their relations

An artist reads colour on three axes at once, the ones Albert Munsell named: hue, value, chroma. [6] Hue is the colour family. Value is how light or dark. Chroma is how clean or grey. Most colour mistakes are chroma mistakes that people think are hue mistakes.

Then the relations:

- **Temperature.** Warm against cool. Not absolute. A cool red is still warmer than any blue, but cooler than the red beside it. Temperature carries space: warm advances, cool recedes, mostly.
- **Chroma distribution.** Where the clean colour is and where the greyed colour is. In most strong images, pure colour is a small percentage, and it sits at or near the focal point. The rest is greyed toward a shared neutral. James Gurney calls the chosen limited region of colour a *gamut*, and the picture's unity comes from staying inside it. [7]
- **Proportion.** Which hue dominates by area, which supports it, which appears as accent. Johannes Itten listed the contrast of extension, meaning proportion, among his seven colour contrasts: hue, light and dark, cold and warm, complementary, simultaneous, saturation, extension. [8] All seven are worth checking on any image.
- **Simultaneous contrast.** What each colour is doing to its neighbours. A grey beside orange turns blue. The artist uses this. A measurement cannot see it, because it is in the eye, not the pixel.
- **Local colour versus perceived colour.** The lemon is yellow. In this light, in this shadow, against this plate, it is painted as a greyed green. The artist paints what is seen, not what is known.
- **Harmony.** Analogous, complementary, split, triadic, monochrome. Name it, then ask whether the harmony is doing the work or merely present.

### 2.5 Light

Colour is the result of light. Read the light before trusting the colour.

- **Source.** Where it comes from, how many sources, whether natural or artificial.
- **Quality.** Hard light with sharp shadows, or soft light with gradual ones. Hard light is drama. Soft light is intimacy.
- **Colour of light and colour of shadow.** Warm light throws cool shadows, and the reverse. If the shadows are the same hue as the light, the image is lit from nowhere.
- **Atmosphere.** Whether distant things go lighter, cooler, and lower in contrast. This is aerial perspective and it is how depth is felt rather than drawn.

### 2.6 Form, space, depth

- **Overlap.** The surest cue of depth. What sits in front of what.
- **Size and placement.** Smaller and higher usually means further.
- **Perspective.** One, two, three point, or none. Whether the picture obeys it, bends it, or refuses it.
- **Flatness.** Some images want to be flat. Flatness is a choice, not a failure.

### 2.7 Line, mark, texture, surface

The hand of the maker.

- **Line quality.** Fast or slow, thick or thin, continuous or broken, searching or certain.
- **Mark.** Brush, pencil, knife, pixel. Whether the marks are hidden or shown. Whether the surface declares how it was made.
- **Texture.** Real texture, in the material, or depicted texture, in the image of a material. Rough against smooth as a contrast like any other.
- **Facture.** The overall character of the surface: thin and glazed, thick and worked, flat and printed.

### 2.8 Rhythm, pattern, repetition

- **Repetition with variation.** The same shape, colour, or interval recurring, never exactly. This is what makes an image feel composed rather than arranged.
- **Interval.** The spacing between repeated things, and whether it accelerates, slows, or stays even.
- **Movement.** The directional energy of the whole. Diagonal, circular, vertical, still.

### 2.9 Meaning and mood

Now interpret, then judge.

- **What it feels like**, in one or two words, before you explain why.
- **Which formal choices cause that feeling.** Low key plus soft edges plus a narrow warm gamut equals one mood. High key plus hard edges plus a cold complementary pair equals another. Trace the feeling back to the dimensions above.
- **Whether the choices agree.** When the subject, structure, value, colour and mark all point the same way, the image has character. When they argue, it has a problem, or a tension the artist meant.
- **Judgement.** Does it do what it set out to do? That is the only fair question, and it can only be answered after the other eight.

## 3. The worksheet

Run this on one image. Write short. One line per field.

```text
IMAGE          <title, maker, date, where you saw it>
VIEWPOINT      <eye level / above / below · inside / outside the scene>

SUBJECT
  literally     <what is there>
  named         <what those things are>
  about         <what the picture is about>

COMPOSITION
  format        <portrait / landscape / square · ratio>
  division      <thirds / halves / golden / diagonal / other>
  focal point   <where, and why the eye lands there>
  eye path      <first → second → third → return or exit>
  figure/ground <what is object, what is space, are the negative shapes designed>
  edges         <where hard, where soft, where lost>
  crop          <what the frame cuts, how close>
  balance       <symmetrical / asymmetrical / radial>

VALUE (squint first)
  masses        <count: 2 / 3 / 4 / 5>
  key           <high / low / full>
  range         <lightest meets darkest at …>
  proportion    <% light / mid / dark, roughly>

COLOUR
  dominant      <hue · value · chroma · % area>
  support       <hue · value · chroma · % area>
  accent        <hue · value · chroma · % area>
  light note    <the lightest chromatic colour>
  temperature   <warm against cool: where>
  chroma        <where clean, where greyed, how much pure colour>
  gamut         <the limited region the colours stay inside>
  harmony       <analogous / complementary / split / triadic / mono>
  simultaneous  <what colours are doing to their neighbours>

LIGHT
  source        <direction, count, natural / artificial>
  quality       <hard / soft>
  light / shadow colour  <warm into cool, cool into warm, or flat>
  atmosphere    <does distance go lighter, cooler, softer>

FORM + SPACE
  depth cues    <overlap / size / perspective / aerial / none>
  perspective   <1 / 2 / 3 point · obeyed / bent / refused>
  flatness      <chosen / accidental>

MARK + SURFACE
  line          <fast / slow · thick / thin · searching / certain>
  mark          <medium · shown / hidden>
  texture       <real / depicted · rough against smooth where>

RHYTHM
  repeats       <what recurs, how it varies>
  interval      <even / accelerating / slowing>
  movement      <diagonal / circular / vertical / still>

MEANING
  feels like    <one or two words>
  caused by     <which of the above>
  agreement     <choices agree / argue / meant tension>
  judgement     <does it do what it set out to do>
```

## 4. From the image to the room

Each dimension has a direct equivalent in a hospitality interior. This is the reason the artist's map matters to the studio. A room is an image a guest walks through.

| What the artist reads | What it becomes in the room |
| --- | --- |
| Subject, three levels | Concept, three levels: what the place literally is, what kind of place it is, what it is about for the operator |
| Viewpoint | The guest's eye height and position at each stop in the arrival sequence |
| Format and division | The plan and the section: proportion of the room, where the big lines fall |
| Focal point | The one thing the eye lands on from the door: back bar, hearth, pass, a single window |
| Eye path | Scene sequencing: street to door to bar to seat, and the route back out |
| Figure and ground | Furniture and fixtures against floor, wall and ceiling; whether the circulation space is designed or left over |
| Edges | Thresholds: hard where a zone should stop, soft where two zones should blend, lost where back of house disappears |
| Crop | What the façade reveals, what the window frames, what a half wall hides |
| Value structure | The lighting plan: where light pools, where it falls away, how many levels of brightness the room has |
| Key | A low-key bar or a high-key café; the first mood decision |
| Colour proportion | Material palette by surface area: dominant field, support, accent, light note |
| Chroma distribution | Where the one clean colour goes, and how much of the room stays greyed toward a shared neutral |
| Temperature | Warm materials against cool, warm lamps against daylight |
| Simultaneous contrast | What the oak does to the plaster beside it; why samples must be seen together, in the room's light |
| Light source and quality | Fixture type and placement: hard downlight for drama, diffuse for intimacy |
| Light and shadow colour | Colour temperature of lamps against the colour of surfaces; whether the room has shadows at all |
| Depth cues | Layered thresholds, overlapping planes, the view through to the next room |
| Mark and surface | Hand-made against machine-made; visible joinery; the facture of plaster, tile, timber |
| Rhythm and interval | Stool spacing, column bays, pendant spacing, booth repetition and where it breaks |
| Meaning and agreement | Character: whether every choice points the same way, so the room knows what it is |

Two of these deserve a note.

**Colour proportion maps onto the studio's measured palette.** The palette pipeline the studio already runs reads source images into six swatches and assigns roles: field, support, accent, light. Those are the artist's dominant, support, accent and light note, measured by area and chroma rather than judged by eye. The artist's worksheet and the measured record describe the same four things. One is relational, one is numeric. Section 5 takes this up.

**Edges are the artist's word for thresholds.** The Seeing Method's second layer, seeing the absence, is about the beat between the host stand and the first table, the moment at the door. In the artist's vocabulary these are edge decisions. Hard, soft, or lost. Naming them that way gives the invisible architecture a drawing tool.

## 5. Three maps, one image

The studio now has three maps of seeing, and they are not the same kind of thing.

- **The architect's map is a structure.** It is 5D and fascia. 5D is the model in which every component carries its own classification, tags, attributes and scenes, so geometry, information, sequence and quantity live in one object. Fascia is the typed, directed links between records that carry change from one to its dependents. Together they say what exists, what it is, and what moves when it moves.
- **The model's map is a path.** It is the read map. A language model holds nothing between calls, so its need per task is not a schema but a route: read first, read next, stop when, write back. Every record it reads carries four spine fields: id, state, a one-line summary, and what it supersedes.
- **The artist's map is a relation.** Nothing in the image has a value on its own. A colour is only warmer or cooler than its neighbour. An edge is only harder or softer than the edge beside it. The artist's method is an order of comparison, whole to parts and back.

Set against the same image, the three read like this.

| Dimension | Artist: the relation | Architect: 5D and fascia | Model: the read map |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Subject | What it is about, under what it is of | What it is for: programme, occupant, the component's classification | The one-line summary; the id and state it carries |
| Composition | Focal point, eye path, figure and ground, edges | Plan, section, scene set; the tag hierarchy that says which things belong together | Read first. The record the route opens before any other |
| Value | Three to five masses, key, where light meets dark | Lighting plan, daylight, levels, scene by scene | Read next, in order of weight. What the route reads second and third |
| Colour | Temperature, chroma, proportion, what each colour does to its neighbour | Finish schedule: material, reflectance, quantity, cost, code | Measured clusters: hue, value, chroma, area share, nearest chip. Written back as a record |
| Light | Source, quality, colour of light and shadow | Fixture schedule, orientation, colour temperature, controls | Estimated illuminant and white point, labelled as an estimate |
| Space | Depth cues, flatness, viewpoint | Dimension, adjacency, clearance, sight line, the attribute on the component | Depth estimate, scale from known objects |
| Mark | The hand and the surface | Detail, junction, tolerance, who builds it | Texture statistics, style label |
| Rhythm | Repetition with variation, interval, movement | Module, bay, grid, the repeated component | Pattern detection, periodicity |
| Change | Move one colour and every neighbour shifts | Fascia: change one record and the link carries it to every dependent | Supersedes: the newer record replaces the older; the route reads the current one |
| Meaning | Mood, then the formal choices that cause it | Intent held under constraint, through to the built result | Stop when. The point at which the route has enough to act or must ask |
| Failure | Taste without structure | Structure without character | Reading without stopping, or stopping without writing back |

### 5.1 The spine, three ways

The read map asks every record to carry four fields. The other two maps already carry them, in their own words.

| Spine field | Read map | 5D and fascia | The artist's image |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| id | Record id | Component id, tag, sheet number | Title, maker, date |
| state | One of four states | Phase, lifecycle, issued or draft | Study, sketch or finished work |
| one-line summary | What the record is, in a line | The component's classification and attribute set | What the picture is about, in a line |
| supersedes | The record this replaces | The revision this replaces, carried by fascia | The study this came from, the version this corrects |

### 5.2 Where to trust each map

The three are not in competition. The model's route can measure what the artist can only compare, and it can be run again tomorrow and return the same answer. The artist can see what measurement cannot: the grey that turns blue beside orange, the edge that lets the eye slide, the lemon that is really about waiting. The architect's structure holds both inside a budget, a lease and a code, and carries a change from a decision to every drawing it touches.

So the working rule is: the read map for the record, the relation for the decision, the structure for the build. A measured palette goes in the record. The choice of which measured colour becomes the field and which becomes the accent is a relation, and it stays with the person. Once chosen, the finish is a component in the model, and fascia carries it to the schedule, the sheet and the spec.
