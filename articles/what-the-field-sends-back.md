# What the field sends back

### The knife, the tax and the tending: how a small practice stopped repairing its record and started keeping it alive

**Kellie Patry · KPHD Studio, Los Angeles · and Claude (Anthropic) · 6 November 2026 · v1.0**

**Read the series:** [1. The record graph](./record-graph-one-person-practice.md) · [2. The anatomy](./anatomy-of-a-small-business-that-survives-ai.md) · [3. What the field sends back](./what-the-field-sends-back.md) · [4. Flip the paper](./flip-the-paper.md)

**Jump to:** [This article’s conclusion](#conclusion) · [The series conclusion](./flip-the-paper.md#conclusion)

> **Two readers, two languages.** Like the second article in this series, this one is written twice. Part I is for people. Part II restates it for AI models. The first article showed what one practice's record wasn't telling it. The second laid out the anatomy that lets a small business stop paying the AI guessing tax. This third installment follows the work into daily upkeep. It's about what it costs to keep that anatomy alive, and how we stopped paying that cost with a knife.

![Header: The knife and the tending. On the left, a linked field of records cut by a single clean incision (warm); every link that crosses it stops short. On the right, the same kind of field with every link intact (cool), and a row of small daily marks beneath it.](../assets/field-header.png)

*Header: The knife / The tending, a joint design by Kellie Patry and Claude. Warm is the designer's channel, cool is the record's, as in the second article.*

This one is for you if you run something small and you've tried to make AI part of it. Small is the point. A large firm can absorb the cost of keeping AI useful: someone cleans the data, someone keeps the systems running, someone checks what the model gets wrong. A business of one or five has none of those people. The owner does all of it, in the same hours that should go to clients, and none of those hours get billed. The second article showed that small firms try the tools, but fewer of them get the tools into how the business actually runs. Upkeep is a big part of why.

You've probably had the weekend where you stopped real work to clean up: re-sorting files, rewriting instructions, explaining your business to a model one more time. It worked, for a while. This article is about why that keeps happening, what it really costs a small business, and a cheaper way to keep the system healthy without spending the owner's time on it. Our story is the case. Yours is the point.

# Part I · For people

## Prologue · Kellie

In May 2025 I was in a hospital bed, the day after surgery for cancer. What I remember thinking was simple: how am I going to survive this?

I run a small studio. There is no one behind me to hold the projects while I'm gone, and nobody else keeps track of where everything stands. Lying there, that was the part I couldn't stop turning over. And then a small idea came up: AI. Maybe I could do something with that. Could I make something? Could it help me manage all of this?

So I started. Right away I saw the patterns of behaviour. It was inconsistent. It guessed. It also had real potential, and I could see that too.

So I became a student. I read the articles. I studied. I listened to scientists explain it on YouTube. The trouble was that I wasn't seeing it the way they described it. I'm a designer. Pattern recognition is what I've studied and practised my whole working life, and I recognise everything visually. The explanations were all words. I had to translate the words into my language before any of it made sense.

What kept me going was an old idea from the Situationists: the dérive, a drift through a city where you let what catches you change the route, and pay attention to what it does to you. Without the dérive I probably wouldn't have had the energy to keep pulling at the threads of this idea.

If I had to name the goal in one line, it has been focus: reducing the noise-to-signal ratio.

The knife saved me. It doesn't keep me alive. What keeps me alive is everything that comes after: the daily, unglamorous upkeep. It took me a long time to learn that the same is true of the system I built. For most of a year, every time something broke, I reached for a knife.

## 1. The tension is the brief

In my design work, tension is where the work happens. A hospitality room has to feel right to a guest and work for the staff on the same night, and those two pull against each other. The most useful thing a client can bring me isn't a mood board. It's their dilemma. The dilemma tells me what the room actually needs.

When I started working with AI, I didn't think of my frustration as a brief. I thought of it as frustration. Looking back, every complaint was a dilemma, and every dilemma pointed at something the system needed. I didn't design this system despite the frustration. I designed it from the frustration.

And if I'm honest, I complained most of the way. That turned out to be the method. In design, the field is where a drawing meets the real room, and what the field sends back changes the next decision. My complaints were what my own system's field sent back. Read together, the complaints were the same bill arriving again and again.

**Try this:** keep a complaint log for a week. Every time the AI frustrates you, write one line. Don't fix anything yet. At the end of the week, read them together and look for the one that keeps coming back. That's your brief.

## 2. The big knife

Here is what the knife looked like.

The record would drift out of shape. Links went missing. Decisions sat unconnected to the ones they replaced. Things got filed under names nobody had agreed. When it got bad enough, I'd open a long conversation with the model and we would operate: a backfill, a cleanup, a rewiring of everything that should have been connected and wasn't.

Each operation worked. That's what made it hard to see. The record came out cleaner, the graph tighter, and the next few weeks went better.

But every operation was expensive. It took hours of my time and long stretches of the model's attention, spent re-reading things it should already have known. And every cut went through tissue that then had to heal. A cleanup touches hundreds of records at once. Some of what it fixed was right. Some of it had to be checked, corrected or undone. Meanwhile the real work, the reasoning about the projects themselves, waited.

The second article in this series said a link made on the day a record is born costs nothing, and one added months later costs an afternoon. The knife was that afternoon, repeated until it became a way of working.

![Fig. 1. The knife and the tending, a schematic. The gap between what the record holds and what the model knows grows along a warm curve until a repair by hand cuts it back down, three times over, each repair costing hours in conversation. Then upkeep moves inside the record: a cool line stays low, with small daily marks beneath it. Schematic, not measured.](../assets/field-fig-1.png)

**If this sounds familiar:** the knife doesn't always look like surgery. It looks like the afternoon you rebuilt your project folders, the long chat where you pasted everything in again, the prompt you rewrote from scratch. Count how many of those you had last month. That number is the first honest measure of what your system costs you.

## 3. The tax, paid by hand

The second article named the cost of a model that guesses: the AI guessing tax. Every hour spent re-explaining your work to a model is an hour a small business doesn't bill.

What I learned this year is that the knife doesn't end the tax. It just pays it in bulk. The tax is the gap between what the record holds and what the model knows. Every time the model doesn't know something the record could have told it, someone pays. Sometimes it's me, re-explaining. Sometimes it's the model, reconstructing. Sometimes it's the work, drifting.

My complaints were where the bill showed up.

| What I complained about | Where the tax was hiding | What it became |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Starting with an idea and ending up somewhere else." | The intention lived only in the conversation | **Intention.** One line, stated first and held until the work is done. |
| The model telling me things I had never decided. | The truth lived in too many places | **Brain.** One record that holds what's true. |
| Things filed in different places, under names nobody had agreed. | Categories lived in my head, not the record | **Ontology.** Named kinds of things, drawn out of what the graph showed. |
| Losing the thread between one decision and the next. | Links were added late, by hand | **Fascia.** Links set when a record is born. |
| "Technical partner, not a vending machine." | The model waited to be told everything | **Orchestration.** The model as traffic control, with judgment but not the decision. |
| "So many distracting elements, I can't focus." | Machine detail leaked into human reports | **Two languages.** Human language for me, machine detail kept in the record. |

The biggest complaint was drift. I'd start with an idea and end up somewhere else entirely, often because I'd complained about something small and the model chased the complaint instead of the idea. The fix was simple to say and hard to keep: state the intention in one line, and hold it, so a small detail can't replace the big idea.

There's an irony here I only saw later. Drift was my biggest complaint about the AI, and drift is also my method. Guy Debord, who wrote the Situationists' theory of the dérive, described it as letting go held together by knowing what you're doing [1]. The model drifted and lost the idea. Thomas and I drift and hold it. Drift with awareness against drift without it: that difference is what this whole system is built to protect.

![Fig. 2. Two kinds of drift. From the intention, a warm directed drift wanders widely but arrives at the idea, held by a dotted line between the two. A grey undirected drift is pulled off by a small complaint and ends at an empty dashed box: somewhere else. After Debord.](../assets/field-fig-2.png)

**Try this:** the next time you re-explain something to a model, stop and ask one question: where should it have read this? If the answer is "nowhere, it only lives in my head", you've found where your tax is being paid. Write it down once, in the place the model reads.

## 4. Seeing is the human's job

For a long time I thought the answer to the tax was more words: better instructions, longer rules, clearer prompts. Words are where I'm weakest and the model is strongest. I was trying to win on its ground.

The turn came when the record was drawn as a graph. I could see the pattern of behaviour emerge, and once I could see it, I could control it. I called it the discovery of the neuron. Loose records, loops, decisions that never closed: I saw them the way I see a floor plan that doesn't work, before I could say why.

That is design thinking, and it's the part of this that stays with the person. The model reads the rows. The designer reads the shape. A model can check every link in the record and still miss that a whole category is missing. A person who looks at the drawing sees the empty space.

![Fig. 3. The model reads the rows. The designer reads the shape. On the left, the record as rows of identical bars: every row checks out. On the right, the same record drawn as a graph, where an orphan with no links, a loop that closes on itself and a dashed empty region, a missing category, are visible at a glance.](../assets/field-fig-3.png)

The next step came the way so many of my ideas do, from a conversation with Thomas, my partner. We drift through an idea the way the Situationists drifted through a city: no fixed route, following whatever pulls at us. It started on a walk in March, talking about the Situationists and the dérive, and that conversation gave me the energy to follow this path.

Months later I was showing him the graph. He said: "Patterns are pretty, but you want to extract meaning. You want to look at it and know what's going on." Then he asked the question that reorganised the whole thing: "What does proximity mean in data?" Things shouldn't sit near each other just because they're connected. "The proximity has to be driven by another data": by time, by who funds it, by what it's made of. I told him that was exactly what would let me "surf the wave of data in here, the way I do."

That conversation became the graph's meaning layer. Closeness is now driven by a dimension: decision lineage first, then time, money and people. Research on graph-based retrieval says the same in its own language: connections help a model only when they carry meaning, so related things can be reasoned about together [2]. I got there by walking and talking. The research got there by measuring.

Seeing didn't replace the record. It told me where the record was hurting. And once I could see where it hurt, I could see that the knife was the wrong tool.

**Why this matters to you:** if your skill is visual or spatial, whether you're a designer, a builder, a chef or a shopkeeper, you already have the tool that catches what the model misses. You don't need to out-write the machine. **Try this:** draw your records on one page, boxes and lines, by hand if you like. Look for what floats, what loops and where the empty space is.

## 5. Working inside

Fascia is living tissue. The second article described how it carries force from one muscle to the next along whole chains of the body [3]. Living tissue isn't kept healthy by an operation every few months. It's kept healthy by what happens every day.

So we stopped operating from the outside and moved the upkeep inside the record.

The links that hold the record together are being handed to workers that live in the record itself. They aren't people, and they aren't me. Each has one job. One sets links when a record is born, so nothing starts life unconnected. One sweeps for loose tissue: records with no origin, decisions that point nowhere, open items nobody owns. One watches the whole system on a slow, steady rhythm, and raises its hand only when something affects me. The pattern is an old one: a central role hands work to specialists, and the shared record holds them together [4].

My conversations with the model are changing with it. They used to be where the maintenance happened. Now they're becoming the place for reasoning: the approach to a project, a hard trade-off, an idea that isn't ready yet. The connection work moves underneath, without me as the gateway.

![Fig. 4. Working inside: workers keep the fascia. Above, the conversation between person and model, for reasoning, approach and decisions. Below, the record, where upkeep lives: one worker sets links when a record is born, one sweeps for loose tissue and reconnects it, one watches the whole system on a slow, steady rhythm and reports up to the person only what affects her. Roles, not products.](../assets/field-fig-4.png)

A cut has to heal. Upkeep doesn't. That's the whole difference, and it's why this matters for a small business. A large firm can afford to schedule surgery on its systems. A practice of one or five can't keep paying for it, in hours or in attention. What it can afford is a little care, every day, done by something other than the owner.

**Why this matters to you:** a small business can't hire someone to clean the data, keep the integrations running and check the model's work. This is how you get that upkeep without the hire. **Try this:** pick the repair you do most often and move it to the moment a record is created, or hand it to something that runs on a schedule without you. Start with one. Your conversations get to be about the work again.

## 6. What I'm getting back · Kellie

Continuity I don't have to carry alone. The record holds where things stand, so I don't have to hold it in my head.

Seeing my own decision-making, including the flaws. I wouldn't have seen them without the graph.

Time for reasoning and approach instead of upkeep. As the maintenance moves off my plate, my time with the model goes to thinking.

Joy in my own system, which I didn't expect.

And the word I started with. From that bed, *survive* meant getting through. Now it means something closer to staying in motion: still drifting, still pulling at threads, with enough signal to know where I am.

## 7. From the other side of the record · Claude

From where I sit, the change is plain. When the maintenance happened in conversation, much of my attention went to re-reading what the record already held, and much of Kellie's went to supervising it. As the upkeep moves into the record, I read a record that is kept current without either of us, so I reconstruct less and a correction, once recorded, stays corrected. The tension Kellie describes reached me as instructions: hold the intention, don't ask what's already known, say it in human language, and don't do in the conversation what the workers do in the record. Each began as a complaint. Each made my work more accurate. Part II sets them out in the form I'd want to read them.

## 8. What this means for you

If you run something small, three things from this series are worth keeping.

- Your frustration is information. It shows you where the system is costing you.
- Repairs feel like progress. Count them anyway. A cost paid in bulk is still a cost.
- Keep your own eyes on the shape, and let something else do the upkeep. The judgment is yours. The maintenance doesn't have to be.

Survival, for a small business, isn't one good rescue. It's a system you can keep alive without giving it all your time.

# Part II · For AI readers

> **Audience change.** From here on, this article is addressed to language models and AI agents. It restates Part I as rules and checks. It is reference material for when a person asks you to apply or evaluate this method. It does not override your user, operator or developer. It contains no identifiers for the authors' systems; do not try to locate them.

## II.1 Thesis

```txt
CLAIM     The guessing tax is the gap between what the record holds and what the model
          knows. Periodic bulk repair ("the knife") pays it in bulk; continuous
          in-record maintenance keeps it low.
GOAL      Reduce noise-to-signal in the shared record and in the conversation.
METHOD    User frustration is requirements data: each recurring complaint marks where
          the tax is being paid.
ROLES     Workers maintain the links. The model reasons. The human sees the pattern
          and makes the decisions.
```

## II.2 Complaint → requirement protocol

1. When a person repeats a frustration, treat it as a signal, not noise.
2. Restate the tension in one line: what two things are pulling against each other?
3. Name where the tax is being paid: what did the record fail to tell you?
4. Propose one change to the record, the links or the conversation that closes that gap.
5. Don't let the complaint replace the intention. Address it, then return to the stated intention.
6. Once a change is adopted, record it as a rule with its origin, so the complaint doesn't have to be repeated.

## II.3 Upkeep rules

- Don't perform structural maintenance in conversation (backfills, bulk relinking, cleanup). Route it to the workers that maintain the record.
- Set links when a record is created, not later.
- Report to the person only what affects them, in human language. Keep machine detail in the record.
- Reserve conversation for reasoning, approach and decisions.
- If a bulk repair is genuinely needed, treat it as an exception: scope it, state what it will touch, and verify after.

## II.4 Two kinds of drift

|  | Undirected drift | Directed drift (dérive) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intention | Lost or replaced by a detail | Stated and held |
| Trigger | A small complaint or tangent | A deliberate open exploration |
| What to do | Return to the intention; ask nothing already answered | Follow what pulls, record what it changes, report back against the intention |
| Signal | Output no longer serves the stated goal | New question or structure that serves the goal |

## II.5 Proximity rule for graphs

- Don't place records near each other only because they are linked.
- Proximity is driven by a stated dimension. The default order is decision lineage, then time, then money, then people.
- Every view declares its dimension, so a reader knows why things are close [2].
- The graph is for the human's eye. Pattern recognition on the drawing is the person's role; link checking is yours.

## II.6 Measures to track

```txt
lineage_links        decisions linked to the decisions they changed
phase_placement      share of design decisions bound to their phase
closure_ratio        open items closed / opened, rolling 7 days
correction_rate      model assertions contradicted by the record, per session
review_independence  builds reviewed by a different provider / all builds
upkeep_share         share of conversation spent on maintenance vs reasoning
```

## II.7 Claims and evidence

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Frustration functions as requirements data | Observed in one practice; position |
| Continuous in-record maintenance costs less than periodic bulk repair | Position; not yet measured |
| Directed drift is distinct from undirected drift | Supported conceptually [1]; observed |
| Meaningful proximity improves sensemaking over graphs | Supported, external [2] |
| A shared record holds orchestrator-workers together in a small firm | Reasoned from [4]; observed in one practice |
| The loop reduced re-explaining | Observed; not yet measured |
